# Changelog

## Play page: levels fixed (2026-11-10)

The levels of the first version of the play page were all one strength, far above their names (the level called "about 1300" played much too well). The page took them from the engine's `UCI_Elo` option, and Kestrel looks at its node count only every 2,048 nodes: every `UCI_Elo` from 1320 to about 2300 searches 1,000 to 2,000 nodes (depth 4 to 7; in four test positions the release searched 1,564, 1,054, 1,697 and 1,663 nodes at 1320, 1500, 1900 and 2300). The engine is not changed (3.2 is as released); `docs/OPTIONS.md` now says what `UCI_Elo` does below 2300.
**New levels** (`docs/play/levels.js`): twelve levels, "about 500" to "about 2800", and full strength. Kestrel always plays at full strength; a level decides how far it looks (1 to 5 plies, or 4,096 / 16,384 positions for the two strongest), how many of its best moves it keeps in mind (`MultiPV`), how carelessly it chooses among them (a move that looks x centipawns worse than the best is played with the weight exp(-x / temp)) and how often it simply overlooks something (a random move). A level that is clearly ahead plays more steadily, the first moves are played with care, a weaker level answers after at least about half a second, and the thinking-time menu is used by full strength only. A game saved by the first version of the page continues at the level with the same name (which is now much weaker).
**Measured** (`tests/web/levels_lab/`: the same `levels.js` and the same WebAssembly engine as the page; the table is in `docs/BUILDING.md`). Against its own level, judged by Stockfish at depth 12: Level 1 loses a piece or more (300 centipawns) with 13 % of its moves, "about 1300" (Level 5) with 4.9 %, "about 2100" with 1.8 %; the average centipawn loss falls from 132 at Level 1 through 69 at Level 5 to 40 at Level 10. A level beats the one below it by +147 to +246 Elo in games between the two (levels 2 to 9, 150 games per pair or more, about ±55; +191, +325 and +325 for the top three with 60 games). Against Stockfish's `UCI_Elo` settings: Stockfish's lowest setting (1320) is about as strong as Level 6, so Levels 1 to 5 are weaker than anything Stockfish offers; Level 8 is about 1600, Level 10 about 2400 and Levels 11 and 12 about 2800 on that scale. The numbers in the menu are estimates of what players of that rating do, **not** ratings from games against people; a level that feels too strong or too weak can be moved by changing its `temp` and `slip`.
**Tests:** `tests/web/levels_test.js` (the table, reading the engine's lines, the choice of the move), `tests/web/levels_engine_test.js` (every level with the real WebAssembly engine).

## Play page (2026-11-10)

Kestrel can be played in a browser without installing or downloading anything: https://alon-berg.github.io/Kestrel-chess-engine/play/. The engine in the page is Kestrel 3.2 itself, compiled to WebAssembly (`src/kestrel_3_2_wasm.cpp`, made from the 3.2 source by `scripts/make_wasm.py` and compiled by `scripts/build_wasm.py` with Zig). There is no change to the engine and no new release; the download files and their checksums are the ones of 3.2.
**Same engine:** the standard bench (8 positions, depth 12) searches 859,322 nodes, node for node like the native programs, and a search from the start position to depth 12 searches 63,397 nodes in both (`tests/web/wasm_bench_test.js`). The WebAssembly build has one thread and no AVX2 (it uses WebAssembly SIMD where the browser has it) and runs at about a sixth of the speed of the AVX2 program on the same computer (about 190 knps in Chrome's engine against 1.1 Mnps natively on my laptop). It has no tablebases, no opening book, no pondering and no `go infinite`; the page varies the first moves itself with `MultiPV`.
**The network** (76 MB) is loaded once and kept in the browser's cache storage. To keep the download small it is split into low and high bytes and gzip-packed (`scripts/pack_net.py`: 44.2 MB instead of 76.3 MB), and the page checks the SHA-256 of the unpacked data against the one of `kestrel_net_3_2.bin` of the release.
**The page** is plain JavaScript without libraries: `rules.js` (the rules, SAN and PGN; tested against python-chess on 29,003 positions with 599,556 moves and against the six standard perft positions, `tests/web/rules_test.js`), an SVG board with click or drag and drop, twelve levels and full strength (the levels of the first version of the page used `UCI_Elo` and turned out to be all one strength: see the entry above), take back, flip, resign, PGN copy and save; the game is kept in the browser's local storage.

## 3.2 (2026-11-08)

A smoother time management for searches with a clock, and four small changes: huge-page advice for the hash table (Linux), a memory limit for `Hash` (macOS), a time reserve that grows with the thread count, and release builds that no longer pay for switched-off options. The network, the search and the evaluation are those of 3.1: with a fixed depth, node count or move time Kestrel 3.2 plays exactly like 3.1 (the same depth-12 bench, 859,322 nodes; 24 test positions searched to depth 14, and with `go nodes 30000`, give identical nodes, scores, best moves and principal variations in the release builds of both).
**Time management (searches with a clock only):** after every iteration of the iterative deepening the engine decides whether to start the next one. 3.1 compared the elapsed time with one of three fixed fractions of the planned time (60 % normally, 90 % when the best move had just changed or the score had fallen by more than 25, 40 % when the best move had been the same for four iterations). 3.2 multiplies the planned time (about 1/28 of the remaining time plus 3/4 of the increment, unchanged, as are the hard limits) by 60 % and by factors that change continuously: 150 %, 130 %, 110 %, 90 %, 70 %, 60 % for 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 or more iterations in a row with the same best move; up to +80 % when the score of this iteration is lower than that of the one before (0.8 % per centipawn of the fall), and up to +50 % (or -10 %) when it is lower (higher) than the score of the previous search of the same game; and 80 % when the best move got more than 90 % of the nodes of the iteration and the score did not fall. It is the option `K32Flags` bit 8 of the development builds (the constants are `T_Tm...`); the release builds have it switched on. Against the packaged 3.1 (Windows x86-64-v3, one thread, Hash 64, no book, the same openings for both sides): **+11.2 ± 6.2 Elo at 3 s + 0.03 s per game (4,000 games) and +6.2 ± 7.5 at 5 s + 0.1 s (2,000 games), together +9.2 ± 4.8 Elo (6,000 games)**. The speed is that of 3.1 (the same depth-12 bench in the same time), so the gain is that of the time management; development matches of the same executable with the rule switched on or off gave +7.3 ± 6.1 (4,000 games at 3 s + 0.03 s) and +0.9 ± 7.5 (2,004 games at 5 s + 0.1 s). Games lost on time in the two release matches: 3 s + 0.03 s: 3.2 5, 3.1 9; 5 s + 0.1 s: 3.2 0, 3.1 0.
**Linux:** the hash table (256 MB or more) is advised with `madvise(MADV_HUGEPAGE)` to use transparent huge pages, so that the random probes of many threads miss the TLB less often; it is an advice and ignored where huge pages are off. I could not measure the effect (no Linux machine); Stockfish does the same. **macOS:** a `Hash` request above three quarters of the installed memory is cut to that size, as 3.1 did on Windows and Linux (3.1 did not know the memory size on macOS).
**Many threads:** the time that is held back for the communication with the GUI grows by 1 ms for every 8 threads above 8 (15 ms with 128 threads), on top of the `Move Overhead` option, because stopping dozens of threads takes longer; with 8 threads or fewer nothing changes.
**Source:** `src/kestrel_3_2_base.cpp` (11,300 lines, 8 copies of the search) is the file to read; `src/kestrel_3_2.cpp` (4.6 MB, 128 copies) is generated from it by `scripts/make_threads.py` (the script also still makes 3.1 from the 3.0 source). The search copies carry more switched-off options for development builds: a pawn-structure history, correction histories by the previous move, by the minor pieces and by the major pieces, and skipping ProbCut when the transposition table says it cannot work.
**Release builds:** the switches and search constants that are options of the development builds (`K22Flags`, `K31Flags`, `K32Flags`, `V31Flags`, `CorrFlags`, `T_...` and a few more) are compile-time constants in the release source (`KVAR` in `src/kestrel_3_2_base.cpp`), so the compiler removes every switched-off idea from the search. With the checks of the new switches as ordinary variables the 3.2 exe was 4 % slower in the depth-12 bench than 3.1 (720 ms against 690 ms for the same 859,322 nodes); with constants it is as fast as 3.1 and 0.5 MB smaller. The options that 2.2 - 3.1 accepted but did not list in `uci` (`K22Flags`, `V31Flags`, `CorrFlags`, `EvalCache`, `QsTT`, `EvalFlags`, `T_...`, ...) are now ignored by the release builds, as are the unlisted options of 1.x.
**Tested and not adopted:** at 20,000 nodes per move against the same build without them (stopped early): the pawn-structure history -1.4 ± 13.3 Elo (1,473 games), the correction history by the previous move -1.9 ± 14.0 (1,287 games) and the ProbCut skip -7.8 ± 12.5 (1,735 games). A build with Zig/clang was 7 % slower than the g++ build.
**Tests:** the 20 test scripts of `tests/`, the two audits, the sanitizer verification and the AddressSanitizer + UBSan thread suite pass on the 3.2 files; logs: `logs/threads_3_2.txt`, `logs/repo_tests_3_2.txt`, `logs/v3_2_verify_log.txt`, `logs/audit_probe_3_2_output.txt`, `logs/audit_sem_3_2_output.txt`; the known limits of 3.1 (threads are started for every search, hash tables of 32 GB and more and more than 64 logical processors on Windows untested) are unchanged.

## 3.1 (2026-11-07)

More search threads and a much larger hash, for computers with many cores. The network, the search, the evaluation and the time management are those of 3.0: with one thread Kestrel 3.1 plays exactly like 3.0 (the same depth-12 bench, 859,322 nodes; 24 test positions searched to depth 16, and with `go nodes 30000`, give identical nodes, scores, best moves and principal variations).
**Threads up to 128 (3.0: 8):** the search is compiled once per thread (`docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`); there are now 128 copies, made from the 3.0 source by `scripts/make_threads.py`, which checks that the copies are identical. The default is still 1 thread. A launcher thread starts the helper threads (about 50 - 100 microseconds each, 6 - 14 ms for 127 helpers) while the main search already runs: the first `info` line comes after 0.6 ms (median) with 128 threads, as with one thread (0.5 ms). After `stop` the 127 helpers need 11 ms (median, 20 ms at most) to finish.
**Hash up to 1,048,576 MB (3.0: 4096):** the table size is still a power of two (a request is rounded down: 24,000 MB gives a table of 16,384 MB). On Windows and Linux a request above three quarters of the installed memory is cut to that size, with an `info string` line, so that a mistyped value cannot page the computer to death; if the system refuses an allocation the engine falls back to smaller tables and now says so. Big tables are cleared by many threads: 16 GB are allocated and zeroed in about 1 s and `ucinewgame` clears them in about 1 s (one clearing thread: 4.6 s and 4.4 s on the same computer).
**Fix:** `go nodes N` and `UCI_LimitStrength` stopped as soon as any thread had searched N nodes. With several threads the first helper got there before the main thread had finished depth 1, and the best-ordered move was played (in a won queen-and-knight against king ending the stalemating move: 3.0 with 8 threads in 37 of 40 tries). Now only the main thread's count ends the search; the `nodes` of the `info` lines (all threads) can be larger than N. For a limited-strength engine use one thread.
**Fix:** short move times with many threads. In 3.0 `think()` started all helper threads before the main search began: 127 threads take 6 - 14 ms on a computer with enough processors and 35 - 100 ms and more with fewer processors than threads or a busy computer, which with a hard limit of 45 ms (`go movetime 60`) was most or all of the time, and the first time check of a thread runs at node 0, so a search whose hard limit had passed by then ended without any result and the best-ordered legal move was played. On a quiet 22-processor laptop, in a won queen ending in which a quiet king move is stalemate (visible from depth 2 on), 31 of 60 such searches with 128 threads (5 of 60 with 64 threads) ended before depth 1; with only a rule that a time limit cannot end a search before depth 1 has completed, 18 of 60 stopped at depth 1 and played the stalemating move (64 threads: 3 of 60). Now a launcher thread starts the helpers and the main search begins at once, and a time limit cannot end a search before depth 1 has completed: 0 of 60 at 64 and at 128 threads, last depth 12 (median), and 0 of 30 with 24 busy programs competing for the processors. The code of 3.0 is the same, but 3.0 starts at most 7 helpers. `stop` and node limits are not affected, and with one thread nothing changes. `tests/breakers.py --threads 64` found the problem; new test: `tests/depth1_test.py`.
**Windows:** the launcher thread that starts the helper threads runs at a higher priority than the search. With more threads than processors the helpers that were already running kept it waiting: starting 127 helpers took 2.6 s (median) on a 22-processor laptop and up to 20 s in a build without this, 35 ms (108 ms at most) with it, and the main search no longer waits for it at all.
**Memory:** `ucinewgame` and `Clear Hash` clear the tables of the search copies that were started since the last clear only; the pawn table of every copy starts as zeros and is no longer stored in the executable file. The Windows x86-64-v3 exe is 81.6 MB (3.0 built the same way: 88.4 MB). Every search copy reserves 6.3 MB of address space, so the process commits about 0.84 GB when idle against 79 MB for 3.0; a one-thread game uses about the same resident memory as in 3.0 (97 MB against 67 MB with a 16 MB hash). Compiling the file takes longer: about 136 s and 1.1 GB with g++ -O3 (3.0: 16 s, 0.5 GB), see `docs/BUILDING.md`.
**Strength with more threads:** on my laptop (22 logical processors) 8 threads score **+123 ± 24 Elo** against 1 thread of the same build at 10 s + 0.1 s per game (200 games with a development build: 85 won, 17 lost, 98 drawn) and **+108 ± 38 Elo** with the release exe (100 games: 38 won, 8 lost, 54 drawn); all games finished normally. More threads than the computer has processors do not help: 64 and 128 threads on the same 22 processors scored +11 ± 71 and +11 ± 57 against 1 thread (32 games each), and the 128-thread engine lost one game on time; use at most as many threads as there are logical processors, and raise `Move Overhead` to 50 - 100 ms with 64 or more threads. I could not test a computer with many more processors. Lazy SMP does not scale linearly: on this laptop (6 performance cores with two threads each, 8 efficiency cores, 2 low-power cores; 76 MB of network weights that all threads read) the nodes per second grow 2.9 times with 8 threads and 3.7 times with 20, and the time to reach a fixed depth does not shrink, because every thread runs slower when the others are busy. Counted in nodes, the search needs 1.4 / 1.8 / 3.0 / 3.7 / 4.4 times as many nodes in total to finish depth 20 with 2 / 4 / 8 / 16 / 20 threads, and 5.1 / 7.3 / 9.2 times with 32 / 64 / 128 threads (these three on 22 processors); if every thread ran as fast as a thread of the one-thread search, the time to depth would shrink by about 1.4 / 2.3 / 2.6 / 4.4 / 4.5 / 6.3 / 8.8 / 14 times with 2 / 4 / 8 / 16 / 20 / 32 / 64 / 128 threads.
**Tests:** `tests/stress_stop.py` with 16, 64 and 128 threads, `tests/depth1_test.py` (new: short searches with 128 threads must still search), `tests/breakers.py` (new: positions that break engines, see `tests/README.md`) with 1, 8 and 64 threads and with 16 threads and tablebases, and the other test scripts all pass; an AddressSanitizer + UBSan build of the 128-copy source passed the thread stress tests at 2, 8, 16, 32, 64 and 128 threads, the UCI fuzzer, `tests/breakers.py --quick` with 8 and 64 threads and the hash tests. Only `tests/edge_test.py` stops there, at two undefined-behaviour spots of the FEN parser that are in 3.0 as well and not changed: a FEN with a rank that does not add up to eight files, and a halfmove clock of 2^31 or more (harmless in the normal build). Logs: `logs/threads_3_1.txt`, `logs/repo_tests_3_1.txt`, `logs/v3_1_verify_log.txt`.
**Known limits:** the threads are started and stopped for every search (a thread pool is not implemented): with 128 threads on a 22-processor laptop `go movetime 60` took 50 ms in the median but once 328 ms in 60 searches, and about 0.8 s (median) when 24 other programs kept all processors busy; on a computer with 128 processors starting 127 threads takes about 14 ms. With a clock and many threads use a `Move Overhead` of at least 100 ms. The table sizes of 32 GB and more could not be run on the 31 GB computer where this was made (the index arithmetic is 64-bit everywhere); on macOS the engine does not know the installed memory and does not cut a `Hash` request; Windows before Windows 11 / Server 2022 confines a process to one group of at most 64 logical processors, so on a machine with more, threads beyond 64 would share those 64 (not tested); the Linux and macOS builds were compiled with Zig but could not be run where they were made (the GitHub Actions workflow runs the published files).

## 3.0 (2026-11-05)

A much stronger network, Syzygy tablebases, and two small fixes. Against 2.3 it scores +130 ± 8 Elo over six time controls (3,800 games, one thread), and +104 ± 11 at 5 s + 0.1 s per game or longer (1,600 games).
**Network:** same architecture as 2.x (39.1 million parameters, 24,576 inputs per side, 1,536 accumulator neurons, 16 -> 32 -> 1 with 8 output buckets), so the speed per node is almost the same (5 % fewer nodes per second, but a smaller search tree), but it was trained again from scratch for 62 hours in two stages on about nine times as many positions. The 2.x network had overfitted its 238 million positions of the Lichess evaluation database; 3.0 also learns from the positions along the best lines stored in that database (2.36 billion positions, mined in two batches) and from 6.6 million positions of its own games. On held-out positions its error is 17 % lower than the 2.3 network's on Lichess positions and 32 % lower on positions from Kestrel's own games. In a match of the two networks inside the same engine (20,000 nodes per move, 2,000 games) the new network scores +138 ± 11 Elo; the engine as a whole scores +130 ± 8 Elo against 2.3 in timed games (3,800 games, six time controls, one thread).
**Syzygy tablebases (new):** the options `SyzygyPath`, `SyzygyProbeDepth`, `Syzygy50MoveRule` and `SyzygyProbeLimit`; WDL probes inside the search, DTZ probes at the root (in a won position only the fastest wins are searched, so the fifty-move budget is not used up); `info` lines carry `tbhits`. The probing code is Fathom (Ronald de Man, basil00, Jon Dart; MIT licence, notice in `THIRD_PARTY_NOTICES.txt`), folded into the single source file. Without tablebase files nothing changes. Tested with the 3-4-5-men files (`tests/tb_test.py`, `docs/SYZYGY.md`); a 600-game match with the tables against the same build without them scored +19.7 +/- 17.5 Elo. 6- and 7-men files use the same code but were not tested.
**Windows:** the engine now asks Windows not to treat it as a background process ("efficiency mode", EcoQoS). A GUI that starts the engine hidden could make Windows slow it down; opting out gave +13 to +17 % nodes per second at 2 to 8 threads in a test.
**Fix:** `info nodes` and `info nps` added the stale node counters of idle helper threads when `MultiPV` was above 1 (that search runs on one thread) or when fewer threads than requested could be started.
**Tests:** the semantic audit `tests/audit_sem.py` accepts a quiet winning move first in its check "promotion to queen when winning" if the principal variation promotes within its next two moves (the 3.0 network plays Ka2 before a8=Q in 8/P6k/8/8/8/8/8/K7; both win, and in 600 such won positions no delayed promotion threw the win away); `tests/race_test.py` now waits for `readyok` before it starts the `go infinite` / `stop` race (a slow start-up of the 76 MB exe was reported as a hang), and `tests/tb_test.py` is new. The search, the time management and every other option are those of 2.3 (depth-12 bench 859,322 nodes with the new network).

## 2.3 (2026-11-02)

Bug-fix release for two defects present since 2.0; strength and speed are unchanged (depth-12 bench still 1,051,844 nodes, same time; a 400-game match against 2.2 scored +7 +/- 21 Elo).
**Evaluation overflow:** the second layer of the network summed 1,536 products in a 32-bit integer; for a few strongly negative neurons of the output bucket for 5 pieces or fewer the exact sum falls below -2^31, wrapped around, and the neuron was read as fully on instead of off (9 of 797,651 evaluations in the depth-12 bench; found by UBSan on Linux). The sums are now accumulated in float32; the AVX2 and the plain code still agree exactly.
It also contains the option fix described under 2.2 (that version was tagged but not released).

## 2.2 (2026-11-02, tag only)

Bug-fix release: the same engine, network and moves as 2.1 (identical search on 18 positions; the depth-12 bench is still 1,051,844 nodes; a 400-game match against 2.1 scored +9 +/- 18 Elo).
Kestrel 2.0 and 2.1 declared options of the 1.x engines with wrong defaults: `NNBlend` was declared with default 70 but the engine runs at 100, so a program that sent the declared defaults (for example after "reset to defaults") mixed the old hand-written evaluation into the search (-6 +/- 17 Elo in a 600-game match, but a different search). The obsolete and development options are no longer declared and release builds ignore `NNBlend`; `tests/options_test.py` guards against this kind of bug.
The source also contains new search ideas and the search constants as switched-off options (`K22Flags`, `T_...`, declared only in development builds); none of the ideas helped in 1,200 to 4,000-game tests, see `logs/fast_matches_2_2.txt`.
Added since 2.1 (not part of the binaries): the README for 2.x, `docs/OPTIONS.md`, `docs/ARCHITECTURE.md`, portable test scripts in `tests/` and a GitHub Actions workflow that runs the published binaries on Linux, macOS and Windows.

## 2.1 (2026-11-01)

Same engine, network and search as 2.0 (checked: identical best move, depth, score, node count and pv on 18 positions; a 400-game match of 2.1 on its defaults against 2.0 with the same settings set by hand scored -3 +/- 19 Elo).
Only the default settings changed, for chess-engine testers: `Hash` 256 MB (was 64), `OwnBook` off (was on), `Experience` off (was on). Depth-12 bench: 1,051,844 nodes (2.0: 934,326, the difference comes from the new defaults).

## 2.0 (2026-11-01)

The first Kestrel with one large neural network (39 million parameters, 76 MB, stored inside the exe) instead of two small networks and a hand-written evaluation.
About +37 +/- 11 Elo ahead of 1.1 and +48 +/- 13 ahead of 1.0 over six time controls (1,900 and 1,500 games; `logs/fast_matches_2_0.txt`).
The network is the one of 2.0 beta 1: the long training run overfitted after about 60 hours and a fine-tune on fresh games did not help (-6.7 +/- 11.2 Elo), so the best snapshot was kept.
New UCI options `EvalFile` and `EvalScale`. Verified with the two black-box audits (83 + 31 checks), a 27-check verification with AddressSanitizer / UBSan and a fuzz campaign.
